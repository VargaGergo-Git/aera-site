What is a good sleep score?

English · Magyar · Deutsch

81 or more is a good night on Apple's scale. Better than any single number: a score near your own usual most nights. A run of lower ones is the thing to act on.

How is the score worked out?

Apple added the Sleep Score in watchOS 26, for Apple Watch Series 6 or later, SE (2nd generation) or later and every Ultra. It appears in the Sleep app on your Apple Watch after you wake up, and you can follow it over time in the Health app on iPhone. Apple says the approach follows guidance from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, the National Sleep Foundation and the World Sleep Society, and was built and tested on more than 5 million nights from the Apple Heart and Movement Study. According to Apple's support pages, it is a number from 0 to 100 built from three parts:

  • Sleep duration, up to 50 points. How long the watch estimates you slept.
  • Bedtime consistency, up to 30 points. When you fell asleep, compared with the last 13 nights.
  • Interruptions, up to 20 points. How often you woke and how long you stayed awake.

Apple's Apple Watch User Guide names the bands: Very Low (0 to 40), Low (41 to 60), OK (61 to 80), High (81 to 95) and Very High (96 and up). Apple refined these classifications in watchOS 26.2, so older articles may quote different cut-offs. To get a score at all, Track Sleep with Apple Watch has to be on and the watch has to be worn in bed. Apple does not publish the exact curve behind each part, so this guide does not guess at it. Tapping the score and then the info button shows Apple's own explanation.

What makes a night good?

Enough sleep. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society recommend that adults sleep 7 or more hours a night on a regular basis (Watson and colleagues, 2015). The National Sleep Foundation panel put the usual range for adults at 7 to 9 hours (Hirshkowitz and colleagues, 2015). Needs vary from person to person, which is why how much sleep you actually need is worth reading. Note that hours in bed and hours asleep are not the same thing: time asleep vs time in bed.

The evidence in detail
Half of Apple's score is simply how long you slept.

Unbroken sleep. When the National Sleep Foundation set out what good sleep quality looks like, how long it takes to fall asleep, how often you wake, and how much time you spend awake after first falling asleep were among the main markers (Ohayon and colleagues, 2017). That is what Apple's interruptions part tries to capture.

Regular timing. In more than 60,000 UK Biobank participants who wore a wrist sensor for a week, Windred and colleagues (2024) found that sleep regularity predicted risk of death more strongly than sleep duration did. It is an observational study, so it shows a link rather than proof, but it fits a wider body of work on the body clock. More in does sleep regularity matter more than duration?

Stages are estimates. Apple lists time in each sleep stage among the things that shape sleep quality, but stages are not one of the three scored parts it names. Whatever role they play, keep them in proportion: studies comparing consumer devices with lab sleep tests (polysomnography) find they are better at telling sleep from wake than at naming the stage (Chinoy and colleagues, 2021; Miller and colleagues, 2022). Why compare it with yourself?

A single night moves for ordinary reasons: a late dinner, a drink, a warm bedroom, travel, a hard training day, a noisy street. A score of 65 after a wedding says very little. Five nights in the 60s when you usually sit in the high 80s says much more.

More on bands and sleep debt

The bands are also the same for everyone, while sleep need is not. Someone who does well on 7 hours and goes to bed at the same time every night may sit in the High band most nights. A shift worker or a new parent may rarely get there, and the more useful question for them is whether this week is better or worse than their own usual. Lost sleep also adds up over several nights: what sleep debt is. Why the comparison has to be with yourself: why a watch should compare you to yourself.

My score is low. What do I change?

Try this
  • Give yourself enough time in bed. Duration is half the score. If you want 7 hours asleep, you usually need a bit more than 7 hours in bed.
  • Fall asleep around the same time. Consistency looks back 13 nights, so a steady bedtime pays off within about two weeks, and one late night costs less once the rest are steady. A bedtime window can help.
  • Protect the second half of the night. Common causes of wake-ups are alcohol close to bed, caffeine late in the day, a warm or bright room, and a late heavy meal. Change one at a time and watch the interruptions part for a week.
  • Check the basics. A watch that went flat overnight or sat loose on the wrist can record a broken or short night that was not. Make sure Track Sleep is on and the battery will last the night.

When to talk to a doctor

A sleep score is a summary of wrist estimates, not a diagnosis, and a good score cannot rule out a sleep disorder. Talk to a doctor if you snore loudly, if a partner has seen you stop breathing or gasp in your sleep, if you are sleepy during the day despite enough time in bed, or if trouble falling or staying asleep goes on for weeks. Those are the signs that usually lead to a proper sleep test (Kapur and colleagues, 2017). This page is general information, not medical advice.

Quick answers

What is a good sleep score on Apple Watch?

Apple rates the score from 0 to 100 and names five bands: Very Low (0-40), Low (41-60), OK (61-80), High (81-95) and Very High (96 and up). A score in the High band is a good night by Apple's own scale. More useful than any single number is where you usually sit: a week that stays near your own usual is a good sign, even if it rarely reaches the top band.

How is the Apple Watch sleep score calculated?

Apple says the score is built from three parts: sleep duration (up to 50 points), bedtime consistency (up to 30 points), which looks at when you fell asleep over the last 13 nights, and interruptions (up to 20 points), which looks at how often you woke and how long you stayed awake.

Why is my sleep score low when I slept long enough?

Duration is only half of the score. A bedtime well away from your recent nights costs consistency points, and several or long wake-ups cost interruption points. A late night after a run of early ones can pull the score down even when the hours were fine.

Is one low sleep score a problem?

Usually not. A late meal, a drink, travel, a warm room or a hard day can lower one night. Look at the pattern over a week or two. Several low nights in a row, or feeling unrefreshed most mornings, are worth acting on.

When should I talk to a doctor about my sleep?

Talk to a doctor if you snore loudly, if someone has seen you stop breathing or gasp in your sleep, if you are sleepy during the day despite enough time in bed, or if trouble falling or staying asleep goes on for weeks. A watch score cannot rule out a sleep disorder.

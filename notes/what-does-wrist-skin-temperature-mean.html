Wrist skin temperature

2026-09-21

Wrist skin temperature on a watch is how warm your skin is overnight relative to your own usual, not a reading of your core body temperature. One night means nothing. A few nights of drift can mean something.

People see a thermometer icon and assume it knows their fever. It does not. What it watches is skin warmth and how that warmth drifts from your personal baseline.

It is not a thermometer

Your wrist, hands, and feet are where your body lets heat out or holds it in. So the temperature there mostly reflects how open or closed the small blood vessels near your skin are. It is not the deep internal temperature a clinic thermometer reads. The two move for different reasons, and they often move in opposite directions.

Your watch does not show you a degree like 37. It shows a shift from your own nightly average. A reading of plus a fraction means warmer than your personal normal for that time of night. That is why every watch needs several nights of wear before a number means anything. It has to learn your normal first. Most watches want roughly 5 to 21 nights before a shift is worth reading.

Why it reads at night

The watch measures during sleep on purpose. At rest, in a steady bedroom, with little movement, your skin temperature is calm and readable. During the day it is hopeless. Sun, clothing, a warm office, a brisk walk: all of it swamps the signal. Sleep is the one stretch quiet enough to trust.

As you drift off, the blood vessels in your hands and feet open up to shed heat. Your wrist warms a little. That is a normal part of healthy sleep onset, not a sign of anything wrong. Kräuchi and colleagues (1999) showed that warm feet help sleep arrive faster. Warm extremities are part of how good sleep begins.

It pairs with What is overnight breathing rate? and What is sleeping heart rate?: overnight floors that only earn attention against your own established nights. Timing context lives in What is my body clock / circadian rhythm?.

What a sustained shift can hint at

A single warm night is just noise. The real signal is a steady shift across several nights against your established normal. When that happens, the number is worth a glance.

An infection coming on can warm your skin a night or two before you feel symptoms. Mason and colleagues (2022), in the TemPredict study, reported multimodal wearable signals where skin temperature moved alongside breathing and heart rate rather than alone. Mitratza and colleagues (2022) reviewed wearable sensors for SARS-CoV-2 detection in The Lancet Digital Health and treated skin-temperature change as one non-specific early input among others.

The menstrual cycle does it too, running cooler in the early phase and warmer after ovulation. Zhu and colleagues (2021) compared wrist skin temperature with basal body temperature for ovulation detection and found wrist skin temperature useful as a relative fertility signal in that study setting. Hard training, a glass of wine, or a hot bedroom can move the same number. The watch sees that something changed. It never sees which one.

It tells you something shifted, never on its own what shifted. See also What does recovery actually measure? for how overnight signals sit together.

What it honestly cannot do

Your watch cannot tell you your body temperature or whether you have a fever. It only sees relative change in skin warmth. It can flag nights warmer than your usual. It cannot give a clinical reading or name an illness.

And the signal is non-specific. The watch cannot separate a coming cold from a hard workout, a late drink, a stuffy room, or the second half of your cycle. Treat it as one quiet input, not an answer. It only earns your attention as a sustained, multi-night drift from your own baseline, once you have worn the watch long enough to build one. A single reading, or your first days of wear, is not a health signal.

If you feel unwell, trust how you feel and a real thermometer over this number.

Reading the drop

2026-09-28

Heart-rate recovery is how far your pulse falls in the first minutes after you stop. Read it against the same kind of effort, not against every workout in one pile.

When you stop hard exercise, your heart rate starts to fall. That first minute is mostly your nervous system easing off the gas. A quicker drop generally points to better aerobic fitness and a body that bounces back fast. The catch is that the number only means something under matched conditions.

What the drop tells you

Cole and colleagues (1999) showed that heart-rate recovery immediately after exercise predicted mortality in a large clinical cohort. The clinical cut they used was a blunt tool for populations. For day-to-day training, the useful idea is simpler: how fast you come down is a window into recovery and fitness, best tracked against yourself.

Apps often show how far your heart rate falls in the first minute, and sometimes the next. That is the same family of signal Cole studied, pulled into a training context. It is not a second resting-heart-rate chart. Resting rate answers a quieter overnight or seated question. The recovery drop answers what happened right after you stopped.

Compare like with like

Daanen and colleagues (2012) reviewed heart-rate recovery as a monitor of training status in athletes. The drop is shaped by how hard and how long you went, how hot it was, how well you slept, and whether you kept moving or stood still after stopping. A hard, short finish and a long easy run leave the body in different states. Different peak heart rate, different heat, different fuel left in the tank. So the drop after each one is not really comparable.

Heat alone can raise the working heart rate at the same pace, which changes the starting point of the drop. That weather effect is why a July finish and a March finish are poor twins even at the same pace. The heat side of that story is in Why does heat raise my heart rate?

That is why a single reading is best read against efforts of the same shape. Over weeks, a faster drop under the same conditions is a good sign. A slower drop after matched work is worth noticing beside sleep and how you feel.

How to read it honestly

Treat each workout's drop on its own first. Do not line every session up as if they shared a peak. Watch the trend within one kind of session. Your easy days against your other easy days. Your hard finishes against your other hard finishes.

And the reading itself is rough. Wrist optical heart rate lags when your pulse is changing fast, which is exactly the moment this is measured. Treat it as a within-person trend under matched conditions, not a precise clinical number. Pair it with your aerobic base and how you actually feel.

Cole and colleagues (1999) were answering a clinical risk question in a population. Daanen and colleagues (2012) were asking whether the same family of signal can track training status in athletes. Neither paper turns one wrist reading after a mixed week into a grade. The shared lesson for a watch owner is narrower: match the effort, then watch the trend.

If yesterday was a short hill sprint and today was an easy hour, do not argue with yourself about which drop was "better." They are different experiments. If three easy runs in a row show a slower first-minute fall while sleep and heat look ordinary, that is the kind of pattern worth noticing.

Zone structure that shapes those sessions is in What are heart rate zones? The slow fitness ceiling beside the drop is in What is VO2max on a watch?

Compare your easy days to your easy days. The trend, not the single number, is the signal.

Field note

Heat, humidity, and heart rate

2026-09-27

On a warm day your heart runs higher for the same pace, and that can look like lost fitness when it is really the weather. Humidity makes the same temperature cost more still.

A higher heart rate in the heat is the cooling system working, not a step back in your fitness.

Why heat lifts your heart rate

To shed heat, your body sends more blood to the skin. That leaves a little less returning to fill the heart with each beat, so every beat moves slightly less blood. To keep the same amount flowing to your muscles, your heart beats more often. Sweating thins blood volume over time and nudges it higher still.

Coyle and González-Alonso (2001) describe this slow upward creep at a steady effort as cardiovascular drift. Montain and Coyle (1992) showed how graded dehydration worsens that drift. A pace that felt easy in the cool can cost more beats in the heat. Holding your usual heart rate instead costs some pace. Either way the effort is real. The heat changed the price.

Warm airSame pace costs more heartbeats
High humiditySweat cools less, so the same temperature feels harder
Cool, dry, or indoorWeather is rarely the story

Why humidity makes it worse

Sweat only cools you when it evaporates. When the air is already damp, it cannot take on much more water, so sweat runs off instead of lifting heat away. Jenkins and colleagues (2023) separate air temperature from humidity for endurance exercise and show both matter. That is why the same temperature feels far harder on a humid day than a dry one, and why a feels-like read often sits ahead of the air temperature when the air is muggy.

Ely and colleagues (2007) linked weather to marathon performance. Hotter and more humid days tend to slow people down. Judging a July run against a March one is judging the weather, not your form.

Reading a warm-day session

If a warm-day session felt harder or your heart ran higher than usual, the weather is the likeliest reason. Read the effort, not just the number. Give it time. Your body adapts to heat over a week or two. Périard, Racinais, and Sawka (2015) describe heat acclimation: blood volume expands and heart rate for the same work tends to fall as summer settles in. The same warm run often gets easier as those weeks pass.

Compare hot sessions to other hot sessions, and cool to cool. A July average against a March average is mostly a weather report. How hard the session felt subjectively still belongs beside the heart-rate picture. That lives in What is workout effort?

Honest limits

No honest tool should invent how many beats the heat cost you. How hard heat hits depends on your size, your fitness, how used to the heat you already are, and how well you were hydrated, and none of that is on the watch. Stay with the direction and the reason. Leave the false precision alone.

Weather at the start of a session is not the same as minute-by-minute sun or shade. A forest loop and an open road on the same forecast are different days for your skin. Treat the read as the mood of the day, not a lab measurement of it. On cool, dry, and indoor sessions, the air was never the story, and the honest move is to stay quiet rather than force a heat story onto a mild day.

Field note

Leave the miss alone

2026-09-29

You usually should not make up a missed workout by piling its kilometres onto tomorrow. A missed day stays missed.

Catch-up feels responsible. In runners it is often how weekly distance jumps. Nielsen and colleagues (2014) associated excessive progression in weekly running distance with higher risk of running-related injuries. Stacking yesterday's session on top of today's planned work is one fast way to create that jump.

What a sensible week looks like instead

Most lasting endurance plans keep the same quiet shape: most days easy, one quality session, one longer aerobic run. Seiler (2010) and Seiler and Kjerland (2006) described that polarized or pyramidal intensity distribution in elite endurance training. Casado and colleagues (2021) found world-class long-distance performances were best predicted by volume of easy runs plus deliberate practice of short intervals and tempo. Haugen and colleagues (2022) integrated similar characteristics across world-class distance runners.

Easy aerobic work is cheap to recover from and rich in the adaptations that last. Hard work then sharpens what the easy volume built. A plan that stacks quality on quality inside one week is fighting that pattern. How easy, moderate, and hard time usually split is in What is effort mix?

Misses, swaps, and today's body

Moving a session onto another rest day you already held this week is assignment, not extra volume. That is different from doubling up. A streak of showing up still counts attendance without demanding punishment miles. What a training streak actually means is in What does a training streak mean?

Today can still change even when a calendar exists. Vesterinen and colleagues (2016) studied individual endurance prescription guided by heart-rate variability and showed daily readiness-style adjustments can reshape the session without rewriting the whole season. If a day feels wrong, trust that over the plan. Balancing push and rest as one decision is in How do I balance push and rest?

Walk-to-run ladders and other gated progressions should wait until the current stage is settled. One good session should not unlock the next. A missed day is neutral. It is not a debt.

What a plan cannot promise

No training plan prevents injury. Buist and colleagues (2008) tested a graded novice programme against a shorter one and did not find fewer running-related injuries. Slower progression and easy-majority weeks are field practice that keep load from spiking. They are not a medical shield.

A plan also cannot see the weather, your sore knee, or a cold before it reaches your heart. Treat the calendar as a well-supported default, not a clearance.

Leave the miss alone. Keep most days easy. Let tomorrow be tomorrow's session, not yesterday's debt.

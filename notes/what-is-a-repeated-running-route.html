Field note

Named ground you keep coming back to

2026-10-01

A repeated running route is the same whole outdoor run you return to often enough that software can recognize the ground, count how many times you came back, and time it with fixed start and finish gates.

One run is a run. Two overlapping paths can still be coincidence. At three matching runs, the ground earns a name and a history. A short piece inside varied workouts is a different object; see What are running segments?

How recognition actually works

Every outdoor run leaves a GPS trail. Apps fingerprint the cells that trail covered, then ask which other runs overlap those cells. Overlap alone is not enough. The paths still have to stay close if you walked them side by side. Alt and Godau (1995) and Eiter and Mannila (1994) formalized that shape test as Fréchet distance. Ta and colleagues (2017) showed how signature-based joins find similar trajectories at scale. Niemeyer's geohash (2008) and Jaccard's set overlap (1912) are the quieter pieces underneath cell fingerprints.

SignalWhat it decides
Three or more matching whole runsA route exists and can be named
Cell overlap plus tight shape matchSame ground, not a near-miss street
Same loop, opposite directionOne place, two timed histories
Fixed start and finish gatesWarm-up walking does not set your best

The same loop the other way is still one piece of ground. Uphill first and downhill first are honestly different efforts, so best times stay apart. One wobbly day that drifts wide of the usual line can still belong if most of the family matches. Without that, a single noisy recording starts a near-identical second route forever.

What the stopwatch should ignore

Recognition looks at the whole recorded path. Timing should not. Fixed gates on the shared start and finish, with interpolation between the samples that straddle each gate, keep a café stop or a late stopwatch press from deciding your best. Only runs that cover nearly all of the route and a comparable distance enter the timed ladder.

Planning how long a future pass might take is a different question. That lives in How long will this route take me?

Speed at the same heart rate

On ground you keep returning to, you can finally ask whether you are covering it faster at the same effort. Friel's efficiency factor (2023) is the field read: speed per heartbeat on matched work. Speak only when there are enough heart-rate runs, the change clears day-to-day noise, and early versus recent windows sat in comparable weather.

Heat alone lifts heart rate. Jenkins and colleagues (2023) delineated how air temperature and humidity change endurance cost. Cool spring against hot July can look like fitness when it is only weather. When those windows do not match, silence is more honest than a congratulation. The read is a display trend. It should not rewrite recovery, strain, or today's effort call. Heat's everyday effect is also covered in Why does heat raise my heart rate?

The honest limits

Merry and Bettinger (2019) measured smartphone GPS error in urban canyons. Tree cover and tall buildings still blur meters. Grouping thresholds are calibrated judgment, not a published recipe for "your Tuesday loop." No validated standard tells every product how to recognize a runner's repeated routes. Whole runs are noisier than fixed stretches. You may start from a different corner, cut one field, or add a block, and that day may not produce a comparable time.

Loops need a start and finish area that repeats across enough runs. Until a stable gate exists, the route can stay visible without inventing a best. Rename, hide, or fold two near-duplicates when the engine split one road. That is you overruling the fingerprint, not a fitness score changing.

A repeated route is named ground plus a fair clock. It is not a leaderboard and not a second readiness score.

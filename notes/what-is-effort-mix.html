Your effort mix

2026-09-23

Effort mix is how your logged training time splits into easy, moderate, and hard work by heart rate.

Decades of watching elite endurance athletes converge on one surprising shape: most of the time easy, a small share truly hard. Not because easy is comfortable, but because it works.

The three efforts

Minute-by-minute heart rate can be sorted into five personal zones, then folded into three buckets you can plan around. Easy is the two lowest zones, the pace where you could hold a conversation. Moderate is the middle zone, comfortably hard. Hard is the top two, the efforts that leave you breathing deep.

It is simply where your training time goes. No target. No grade. Just the shape of how you actually train. That shape pairs with What are heart rate zones? for the five-band view and with What is Zone 2 training? for the easy aerobic base those low zones are aiming at.

Why mostly easy wins

Seiler and Kjerland (2006) logged elite endurance athletes and found roughly four out of five sessions easy, with the rest genuinely hard. Seiler (2010) reviewed that distribution as best-practice shape across sports. Casado and colleagues (2021) found world-class distance performances best predicted by volume of easy runs plus deliberate short hard work.

Long easy work grows the aerobic engine. The heart pumps more blood per beat. Muscles grow denser capillaries and mitochondria. The body gets better at burning fat. Because easy sessions are cheap to recover from, you can stack a lot of them. Hard sessions then sharpen the engine the easy volume built.

To race fast, train mostly slow. The best in the world already do.

The trap in the middle

The most common everyday pattern is the opposite shape: nearly everything moderately hard. Each session feels productive, but the middle costs real recovery without delivering the volume of easy work or the sharpness of true hard work.

Stöggl and Sperlich (2014) found polarized training (easy plus hard, little middle) moved key endurance variables more than threshold-heavy or high-volume-only blocks in their trial. Stöggl and Sperlich (2015) mapped how well-trained and elite athletes actually distribute intensity. Silva Oliveira and colleagues (2024) reviewed dozens of trials and found polarized and pyramidal shapes roughly tied on most outcomes. Filipas and colleagues (2022) saw both pyramidal and polarized blocks work over sixteen weeks in trained runners. What every winning shape shares is the base: a large majority of time spent easy.

How to read yours

Look at the easy share first. A broad easy stretch is the win, not a sign you slacked. A small hard share is by design. A sliver of true intensity goes a long way. If the middle dominates, your easy days may be running too hot. Slowing them down is the cheapest upgrade in endurance training.

Compare one week to the middle of your last eight weeks, band by band. Your own history is the only fair yardstick. Name a change only when one band sits clearly away from its usual share. Week-to-week wobble otherwise looks identical to a real change. This also sits beside What is workout effort? when how a session felt disagrees with where the heart-rate minutes landed.

The honest limits

The mix only sees logged workouts. A hard day of yard work or an unlogged ride never reaches it. Heart rate lags at the start of intervals, drifts upward on hot days and long efforts, and can make strength work look harder than it metabolically is.

Zones estimated from heart-rate reserve are honest but approximate. The border between easy and moderate is not a lab threshold. And the research itself comes mostly from trained endurance athletes over weeks-long blocks. People respond differently. Treat the mostly-easy shape as a well-supported default, not a law.

Most of your training time should stay easy if endurance is the goal. That is the line worth remembering.

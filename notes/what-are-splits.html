Your splits

2026-09-25

Running splits are equal pieces of a run that show whether your pace held, drifted, or faded, rather than hiding that story inside one finish time.

A finish time alone cannot say where the run got away from you. Cut the distance into equal slices, usually a kilometre or a mile each, and the shape of the effort appears.

What a split is

A split is one slice of the route. Pace for that slice is simply the time it took divided by the distance it covered. Line those pieces side by side and you can see which kilometres were quick, which were slow, and how wide the gap between them was.

Fastest splitYour quickest single piece
Average paceOverall pace across the whole run
SpreadGap between quickest and slowest split

A tight spread means a steady, level effort. A wide spread means pace swung a lot from piece to piece. Neither is a verdict by itself. Hills, heat, and a deliberate hard start all widen the spread.

How the two halves compare

The bigger story is often the two halves. Compare pace over the first half of the distance against the second. Run the back half faster and that is a negative split. Run it slower and that is a positive split, the classic fade. Hold the two halves within a hair of each other and that is even pacing.

Tucker, Lambert, and Noakes (2006) analysed men's world-record distance performances and found the winning shape was almost always even pacing with a strong finish, or a slight lean into a faster second half. Grivas (2025) reviewed the physiology and psychology behind that negative-split pattern for marathon racing.

There is a practical reason the shape works for many runners. Starting conservatively spares muscle fuel early, keeps the body from overheating too soon, and softens the slow heart-rate creep of a long effort. That leaves something in the tank for the finish. Go out too hard and the second half often sags. None of this is a personal judgment. A positive split can come from hills, heat, or simply choosing to start hard. It is one pattern among several, not a mistake.

This sits next to What is pacing? Pacing names the overall shape after hills are counted. Splits are the equal pieces that make that shape visible kilometre by kilometre.

A read on consistency

How much each split wanders from average pace can be turned into a single consistency percentage. Lower means steadier. Higher means choppier. Cuk and colleagues (2024) studied running variability in the marathon and used related pacing measures. Faster, more experienced runners tend to show steadier splits. Recreational runners show more wobble. Treat it as a gentle gauge of how level you kept things, not a grade.

Durability asks a related but different question: whether pace and heart-rate cost held up later in a long effort. That lives in What is durability? Even splits can still cost more heart later. A fade on pace can still look fine on heart cost.

The honest limits

GPS pace is noisy over short windows. Gilgen-Ammann, Schweizer, and Wyss (2020) validated distance recordings across sport watches and found watch distance can be off by a few percent, usually worse while running than walking. A single short split sits inside that error, so one surprisingly fast or slow piece may be the watch, not your legs. Read short splits as indicative, never exact. Trees, tunnels, and tall buildings make it worse. Open tracks read cleanest.

Treadmill and indoor runs have no GPS, so their splits are often spread evenly across time from one summary distance. They look perfectly even by construction and tell you nothing about how you actually paced.

A structured session is another kind of story. The question there is whether each rep held, not whether kilometre two beat kilometre one. That lives in What are interval workouts?

The steady shape is a general guide, not a law. The best pattern varies with fitness, experience, terrain, and the day. Splits show where the run went. They do not grade you.

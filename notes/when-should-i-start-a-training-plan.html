When your week already has a rhythm

2026-09-30

You should start a training plan when your own week already shows a steady rhythm, not when a calendar is empty and hope is loud.

A plan does not manufacture motivation. Timely suggestions help people act on a behaviour they already intend, and they do little for a behaviour that is not there. Klasnja and colleagues (2019) tested contextually tailored activity suggestions in the HeartSteps micro-randomized trial. Nahum-Shani and colleagues (2018) summarised the same idea for just-in-time adaptive interventions: timing and rarity keep prompts welcome. Frequent or pushy prompts drive people to tune out.

What "ready for a plan" usually looks like

A useful threshold is boring and personal. Roughly three or more sessions a week, held across most of the last month, with something recent enough that the habit is still warm. That is a description of you, not a target copied from someone fitter.

What a plan is for

A plan's real job is shape. Mostly easy days, one harder day, one longer day, and recovery weeks, so the work you are already doing adds up instead of repeating. How easy, moderate, and hard time usually split is in What is effort mix?

In recreational endurance athletes, planned variation of volume and intensity across weeks tends to build fitness more reliably than the same effort repeated, and it is linked to better adherence than training with no plan at all (Bradbury and colleagues, 2020). The honest caveat: the effect is real but modest. A plan helps you act on a habit you already have. It does not create one.

Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006) showed that spelling out when and where you will act raises follow-through on intentions. Dai, Milkman, and Riis (2014) described how temporal landmarks can restart aspirational behaviour. Neither finding says a blank calendar plus a download equals fitness.

Fresh-start moments such as a Monday or a new month can make a plan feel urgent. Use them as a calendar cue only after the rhythm is already there. A landmark without a habit is still an empty week with nicer stationery.

Structure, not pressure

Self-determination work in health interventions (Ntoumanis and colleagues, 2020) keeps autonomy in view. Declining a plan, pausing one, or brushing past a single offer should cost nothing. A suggestion that arrives at the wrong moment, or arrives every day, stops being a suggestion.

A plan is not a prescription, a judgment of the weeks you did not train, or a promise about injury. If a day feels wrong, trust that over any calendar. Balancing push and rest as one decision is in How do I balance push and rest? Catching up a miss by stacking kilometres is usually the wrong move; leave the miss alone in Should I make up a missed workout?

Start when your week already has a rhythm. Let the plan give that rhythm a shape. Quietly skip anything that asks you to invent a habit from zero.

Your sleeping heart rate

2026-09-14

Sleeping heart rate is the average pace your heart holds across the night. It is usually lower than daytime resting rate, because posture, digestion, stairs, and daytime stress are mostly off the stage.

If you only remember one sentence: overnight rate and awake resting rate answer different questions, so do not be surprised when they disagree.

Night floor versus daytime rest

Daytime resting heart rate is sampled in calm waking moments. Sleeping heart rate averages the quieter overnight stretch. Many watches fold the night's low stretch into a daily "resting" figure, which is why the number can sit well below what people mean by resting while awake. Why is my resting heart rate up? is the daytime sibling. Keep the labels straight before you chase either number.

LabelWhat it usually samples
Sleeping heart rateAverage overnight pace while you are out
Awake resting rateCalm waking moments, still with daytime context
Watch "resting" that includes sleepOften pulled toward the overnight floor

Shcherbina and colleagues (2017) studied wrist heart-rate accuracy across devices and activities. A still, sleeping body is one of the kinder conditions for optical sensors. That does not make stage charts trustworthy. Are Apple Watch sleep stages accurate? is the separate note on stage labels. Overnight heart numbers are usually sturdier than those colour blocks.

The overnight dip

A healthy night often has a shape. Rate eases down as sleep deepens, finds a valley in the small hours, then climbs toward morning. Boudreau and colleagues (2013) described circadian variation of heart-rate variability across sleep stages. Ben-Dov and colleagues (2007) linked a blunted overnight heart-rate dip with higher all-cause mortality in a cohort study. That is a population finding, not a personal risk score from one flat night. A single flat chart means little. A pattern of nights without a valley is the calmer reason to look at evenings, load, and sleep timing.

What moves the night number

Pietilä and colleagues (2018) studied alcohol's acute effect on cardiovascular autonomic regulation in the first hours of sleep in a large real-world employee sample. Leota and colleagues (2025) reported a dose-response relationship between evening exercise and sleep. Reimers and colleagues (2018) meta-analysed how exercise training lowers resting heart rate over months. Zhang and colleagues (2016) meta-analysed resting heart rate against mortality in the general population. I am citing those papers as they sit in the Field Guide source list. I have not attached a personal "safe drink count" or a magic evening workout cutoff to your chart.

Altitude also lifts resting work. Richalet and colleagues (2024) and Bärtsch and Gibbs (2007) review how thinner air raises cardiac demand, especially in the first days. A higher overnight rate after travel to altitude is often context, not a mysterious crash in fitness.

Overnight rate also feeds recovery and stress reads because a heart that would not settle is a loud background-load signal. What is HRV? is the paired autonomic note. Same night, different question.

The limit, said plainly

The number says something moved. It does not say why. Alcohol, heat, late training, altitude, and an oncoming cold can write similar lines. One night is one night. Patterns worth acting on live across a week. Folklore about exactly when the valley lands as proof of recovery is tidier than the evidence I have in this source list, so I am not selling that story.

Watch the overnight floor against your own usual nights. Treat mixed "resting" labels with suspicion. That is the useful read.
